Research Brief No. 1

Same crime, more prison

Idaho and Utah have almost exactly the same violent crime rate. Idaho locks up its residents in state prison at 2.6 times Utah's rate, and it is now running out of room.

BarNone researchNovember 2026Idaho · Utah · Washington · OregonDownload the data (CSV)

Key findings

What the numbers show

  1. 231 vs. 230

    Violent crimes per 100,000 residents in Idaho and Utah in 2024. Idaho's property crime rate was lower still: 736 versus Utah's 1,409.

  2. 490 vs. 186

    People in state prison per 100,000 residents at the end of 2023. Idaho had the fifth highest rate in the country. Utah's was about half the national rate of 360.

  3. 152 vs. 28

    Women in prison per 100,000 women in 2023. Idaho's rate is the highest in the nation and more than five times Utah's.

  4. 10,023 people, 8,200 beds

    Idaho's prisons are full. About 1,750 people are held in county jails or out of state, at an expected cost of around $51 million this year.

Figure 1

Crime does not explain the gap

If prison rates simply followed crime, states would line up from the bottom left to the top right. Idaho sits far above where its crime rate would put it.

Washington and Oregon complicate any simple story. Both have lower imprisonment rates than Idaho and higher violent and property crime. That is the honest shape of the research: prison has some effect on crime, but the effect is small and shrinks as prison populations grow. Utah shows a state can match Idaho's public safety without matching its prison population.

Figure 2

The gap is widest for women

Idaho corrections leaders say about 80% of incarcerated women are serving time for drug or other nonviolent convictions, and that the root causes of the state's high rate are not well understood. Reporting by InvestigateWest has also found women held past their parole dates while they wait for required programs.

Figure 3

The bill is coming due

  • Idaho pays counties $55 a day per person for the first week and $75 a day after that, plus $2.50 a day for medical costs. Out-of-state beds average about $83 a day.
  • Housing people outside state prisons is expected to cost around $51 million this year, with the placement budget growing just over 69%.
  • The Department of Correction is asking for another $45.3 million to open nearly 1,000 new beds, on top of earlier construction funding.

Read with care

What this brief does not show

  • Correlation is not causation. Comparing states cannot prove that Idaho could cut its prison population without any change in crime. It does show that Idaho's high imprisonment is not explained by high crime.
  • Utah is not Idaho. The states differ in population, urban share, and culture. Utah also passed a major justice reinvestment package in 2015, and its own prison population is now projected to grow.
  • Crime data has gaps. FBI figures depend on which agencies report, and the 2021 switch to a new reporting system disrupted national data.
  • Different counts measure different things. This brief uses sentenced state prisoners. Broader measures that include jails and youth facilities put Idaho at 720 per 100,000 and Utah at 396, the same pattern.

Questions for policymakers

Before Idaho builds more

  • Who is being admitted to Idaho prisons, for what, and for how long, compared with Utah?
  • How many admissions are probation or parole revocations without a new crime?
  • How many people are past their parole eligibility date and still inside, and why?
  • What have mandatory minimums, including the 2024 fentanyl trafficking law, added to the population?

BarNone will pursue these questions through public data and records requests. If you have data or experience that can help, get in touch.

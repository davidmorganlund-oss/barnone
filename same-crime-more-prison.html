Research Brief No. 1 · Idaho

Same crime, more prison

Idaho has one of the lowest crime rates in the West and the highest imprisonment rate among its six neighbors. Utah, with the same violent crime rate, imprisons people at well under half Idaho's rate. This brief looks at why, what it costs, and what Utah did differently.

Key findings

What the numbers show

  1. 231 vs. 230

    Violent crimes per 100,000 residents in Idaho and Utah in 2024. Idaho's property crime rate was the lowest of any state in the country.

  2. 490 vs. 186

    People in state prison per 100,000 residents at the end of 2023. Idaho's rate is the highest of its six neighbors and the fifth highest in the nation.

  3. r = 0.04

    Across Idaho and its six neighbors, violent crime rates and imprisonment rates have essentially no relationship. Crime does not explain who locks up the most people.

  4. 152 vs. 28

    Women in prison per 100,000 women. Idaho's rate rose 15% in a single year, to the highest in the nation.

  5. 87%

    Share of Idaho prison admissions that came from probation or parole revocations and riders, not new direct sentences, in the state's last full analysis.

  6. $100 vs. $9

    Daily cost of a prison bed versus parole in Idaho. The state expects to spend about $51 million this year housing overflow in jails and out of state.

Why Utah?

Choosing a fair comparison

Utah is not part of the Pacific Northwest, so why compare it with Idaho? Because a fair comparison has to hold as much constant as possible. Utah borders Idaho, shares its fast growth, its conservative politics, and much of its culture, and in 2024 had a violent crime rate within one point of Idaho's. If Idaho's prison population were simply a response to crime, Utah's would look similar. It doesn't.

Washington and Oregon, BarNone's other home states, make weaker comparisons on this question because their crime rates are much higher than Idaho's. To make sure Utah isn't a cherry-picked outlier, this brief also compares Idaho with all six states that border it.

Figure 1

Idaho and its six neighbors

If prison simply followed crime, states would line up from the bottom left to the top right. They don't, and Idaho sits highest of all.

Montana and Wyoming come closest to Idaho's imprisonment rate. Montana has nearly twice Idaho's violent crime. Wyoming has slightly less violent crime than Idaho and still imprisons people at a lower rate. Washington and Oregon have far more property crime and far lower imprisonment. Across all seven states the correlation between violent crime and imprisonment is 0.04, effectively zero. With only seven states that number should not be overread, but it points the same way as everything else in this brief: the size of a state's prison system reflects its policy choices more than its crime.

The gap is widest for women

1,296 → 1,518

Women in Idaho state prisons, end of 2022 to end of 2023. That is a 17% increase in one year. Utah went from 442 to 473.

About 80%

Share of incarcerated Idaho women serving time for drug or other nonviolent convictions, according to the Department of Correction, which also says the root causes of the state's high rate are not well understood.

What drives it

Two doors into prison, one slow door out

A prison population grows when more people come in, when they stay longer, or both. Idaho's records point to both.

The front door87% of admissions

In 2012, revocations of probation and parole and "rider" placements, Idaho's program of retained jurisdiction, made up 87% of prison admissions and about two-thirds of the population. Between 2007 and 2011, Idaho's crime rate fell 13% while its prison population grew about 10%.

Council of State Governments Justice Center, 2013
The back doorAt least 463 women

Between 2019 and 2025, the parole commission granted parole to more than 2,400 women. In 19% of cases, the release date was set more than six months after the hearing, often to finish required programs. From January to August 2026, 251 people had parole delayed more than six months, and for more than a third, the only condition was completing assigned programming.

InvestigateWest, October 2026

Idaho tried to address both doors through its 2014 Justice Reinvestment Initiative. The most detailed public analysis of Idaho's admissions we could find dates to 2013. If that is still the latest, it is itself a finding: Idaho is deciding whether to build new prisons without a current, public picture of who is entering them and why.

Sentencing law adds pressure on length of stay. Idaho's 2024 fentanyl trafficking law (HB 406) set mandatory minimum terms of 3, 5, or 10 years depending on quantity, taking that decision away from judges. Its effect on the population has not yet been publicly measured.

Figure 3

The bill is coming due

  • A year in an Idaho prison costs about $36,500 per person. A year on parole costs about $3,300.
  • Housing people in county jails and out of state is expected to cost around $51 million this year, with that budget growing just over 69%.
  • The Department of Correction is asking for $45.3 million to open nearly 1,000 new beds, including a 560-bed women's prison estimated to cost $183 million to build.

Update · November 2026

Building alone won't close the gap

The Legislature will decide the $45.3 million request in its 2027 session. Even if every new bed opens, Idaho would still have hundreds more people than prison beds.

About 1,800 beds short

Idaho's prison population is about 10,000 people. Its prisons hold just over 8,200.

Idaho Department of Correction, via East Idaho News, Sept. 2026
Still about 850 short

The three new facilities add 952 beds. Subtracting them from today's shortfall leaves roughly 850 people without an in-state prison bed, before any further growth.

BarNone calculation from the figures at left and below
  • Women are the tightest squeeze. The state is about 200 beds short for women, so some are held in county jails. The South Idaho Correctional Institution in Kuna, built as a men's prison, now holds nearly 400 women and 300 men.
  • The real question is demand. With a gap of this size, lawmakers will face the same choice again unless fewer people come in or people leave sooner. The questions in Next steps are where that answer lies: revocations, parole delays, mandatory minimums, and women's admissions.

What Utah did

Utah faced the same pressure in 2015

A decade ago Utah looked a lot like Idaho does now. Its prison population grew 18% from 2004 to 2013, mostly because people were staying longer. Two-thirds of 2013 prison admissions were people returned for supervision violations, and 62% of new commitments were for nonviolent offenses. Without changes, the state projected 2,700 more people in prison by 2034.

In 2015 Utah passed House Bill 348. It:

  • Reclassified first and second drug possession convictions from felonies to misdemeanors.
  • Capped jail and prison time for technical violations of probation and parole and required swift, proportionate sanctions instead of automatic revocation.
  • Let people on supervision earn 30 days off for each month of compliance.
  • Redirected nearly $14 million toward treatment, county programs, and supervision.

The reforms were projected to avert nearly all of that growth and save more than $500 million over 20 years. They are not the end of the story: Utah's own projections now show its prison population rising again. But Utah chose to act on the front and back doors before building, and its imprisonment rate today is well under half of Idaho's.

The research

What decades of evidence say about prison and safety

About 5%

Share of the 1990s national crime decline attributed to increased incarceration, with a range of 0 to 10%. In the 2000s, the contribution was essentially zero. Each additional person in prison buys less safety as prison populations grow.

Brennan Center for Justice, 2015
Certainty over severity

The chance of being caught deters crime far more than the length of punishment. Lengthening sentences that are already long adds little deterrence, and prison may slightly increase reoffending.

National Institute of Justice, Five Things About Deterrence

For a state with crime as low as Idaho's, the evidence suggests more prison beds will buy very little additional safety at a very high price.

Read with care

What this brief does not show

  • Correlation is not causation. Comparing states cannot prove Idaho could shrink its prisons with no change in crime. It does show Idaho's imprisonment is not explained by high crime.
  • Seven states is a small sample. The near-zero correlation is suggestive, not conclusive.
  • Utah is not Idaho. The states differ in population, urban share, and how their courts and supervision systems work.
  • Crime data has gaps. FBI figures depend on which agencies report, and the 2021 switch to a new reporting system disrupted national data.
  • Different counts measure different things. This brief uses sentenced state prisoners. Broader measures that include jails and youth facilities put Idaho at 720 per 100,000 and Utah at 396, the same pattern.
  • Some Idaho figures come from news reporting. Cost, capacity, and parole figures are as reported by the Idaho Capital Sun, East Idaho News, and InvestigateWest from Department of Correction and parole commission data.

Next steps

Before Idaho builds more

Idaho's lawmakers should have answers to these questions before approving new prison capacity:

  • Who is coming in? A current breakdown of admissions by type: new sentences, riders, probation revocations, parole revocations, and technical violations with no new crime.
  • Who is staying past eligibility? How many people are past their parole eligibility or tentative release date, and what condition is holding each one.
  • What are mandatory minimums adding? How many people have been sentenced under the 2024 fentanyl law, and for how long.
  • Why women? Admission offenses, revocation rates, and sentence lengths for women compared with men and with Utah.

BarNone will pursue these questions through public data and records requests and publish what we find. If you have data or experience that can help, get in touch.

Methods

How we built this brief

  • Imprisonment rates are sentenced people under state prison jurisdiction per 100,000 residents on Dec. 31, 2023, from the Bureau of Justice Statistics. They exclude jails and federal prisons.
  • Crime rates are FBI Uniform Crime Reporting rates for 2024, the latest year available, as compiled by USAFacts.
  • The comparison group is every state that shares a border with Idaho: Washington, Oregon, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming, and Montana.
  • The correlation is a Pearson coefficient between 2024 violent crime and 2023 imprisonment across the seven states. Without Idaho it rises to 0.37, still weak.
  • Prison and crime years differ by one year because each source's latest release covers a different year.
  • All figures are in the downloadable CSV, with a source for every number. The November 2026 update adds the Department of Correction's facility-by-facility request.
  • How we stay nonpartisan. BarNone works with lawmakers of every party in Idaho, Washington, and Oregon. We use public data, publish our methods and sources, share findings with legislators and staff of both parties, and take corrections from anyone. We do not endorse candidates or parties.

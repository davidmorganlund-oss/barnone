Research Brief No. 4 · Washington

Use it or lose it? Year-end spending at the Department of Corrections

Every June, state agencies face the same choice: spend what's left in the budget or give it back. Thirteen years of state payment records show Washington's Department of Corrections speeds up its equipment and vehicle buying at the end of the year far more than other state agencies do. In a year when the state faces a $4 billion shortfall and DOC was spared from cuts, that pattern deserves a closer look.

The basics

What is "spend-down," and why does it matter?

Washington writes its budget two years at a time. Money an agency doesn't spend by the end of that budget generally goes back to the state. Agencies also know that leftover money can be read as a sign they need less next time. Together, that creates pressure to spend whatever is left in the last weeks before the fiscal year ends on June 30. People in government call it spend-down, or "use it or lose it."

The easiest things to buy quickly are equipment, vehicles, furniture and supplies, so that's where spend-down shows up. It happens across government, and not every June purchase is waste. Some purchases are planned months ahead and simply arrive, or get paid for, at year-end.

But when one agency buys far more in May and June than the rest of the year, year after year, and far more than other agencies buying from the same vendors, it's fair to ask whether those purchases were needed, or whether that money could have gone back to the state for schools, health care or closing a budget gap.

Figure 2 · Vehicles

Same dealers, same state contract, different timing

Vehicles are one of the easiest year-end purchases to explain away: models are ordered in batches and arrive when they arrive. So we compared DOC with every other agency buying from the same eight dealers on the state's vehicle contract. If May and June were ordinary months, they would hold about 17% of a year's payments. For other agencies, they do. For DOC, they don't.

In fiscal 2026, every dollar DOC paid Bud Clary Chevrolet, $277,000, was paid in May or June. The dealership's legal name includes Cadillac, but the public record doesn't show what models DOC bought. The state contract it buys under lists Chevrolet work trucks. We are requesting the purchase orders.

Figure 3 · Tactical gear

Spending on tactical gear and firearms has quadrupled since 2024

DOC's payments to tactical, firearms and law enforcement equipment vendors rose from about $0.9 million in fiscal 2024 to $3.8 million in fiscal 2026. Part of that is a contracted replacement of 1,700 duty handguns. Tactical gear from the other vendors does not appear in DOC's published purchasing plan for fiscal 2026.

What we checked

Some year-end spending is explained. Some isn't.

We checked DOC's largest fiscal 2026 vendors against its posted contracts, its own published purchasing plan for the year, and the 2025–27 budget. Prisoner transport buses, radios and handguns trace back to contracts or budget items. Vehicles, kitchen equipment, flooring and tactical gear do not appear in DOC's plan.

Why it matters now

A shrinking prison system that costs more

  • Spared from cuts. When the governor asked state agencies to propose budget cuts for 2025–27, the Department of Corrections was one of the few agencies exempted.4 Most other agencies were asked to cut at least 6%.5
  • Fewer people, higher cost. Washington's prison population fell by more than 4,000 people after 2019, yet the cost of incarceration went up.6 DOC now reports $86,261 per person per year.7
  • A $4 billion shortfall. The governor projects a gap of about $4 billion in the first year of the next budget and has asked agencies to find up to 15% in savings.8

Year-end spending is not DOC's biggest cost. Staff, health care and overtime are. But it is one of the clearest places to see whether an agency treats leftover money as a budget to use up or a surplus to give back.

What's next

We're requesting the purchase orders

The state's public payment records show who was paid, how much and in what month. They don't show what was bought, or when it was ordered. That is the difference between a delivery that happened to land in June and a decision to spend money before it ran out.

BarNone is filing a public records request with the Department of Corrections for purchase orders, invoices and order dates for the vendors flagged above, and for any internal guidance on spending remaining funds at the end of fiscal 2026. We will publish what we receive as an update to this brief.

Read with care

What this brief does not show

  • Paid is not ordered. Open Checkbook records the month a payment was made. Some May and June payments are for goods ordered months earlier.
  • No item detail. Payment records list vendors and spending categories, not items, models or facilities. Purchase card activity appears only in summary.
  • Year-to-year swings. Single years move a lot. The 13-year averages and the vehicle comparison are more reliable than any one year.
  • A common practice. Year-end spending happens across state government. This brief compares DOC with all other agencies combined to show how far DOC differs, not to suggest no one else does it.
  • Not an accusation of wrongdoing. Nothing in the public record shows that any purchase broke a law or rule. The question is whether the spending was needed.
  • How we stay nonpartisan. BarNone works with lawmakers of every party in Idaho, Washington, and Oregon. We use public data, publish our methods and sources, share findings with legislators and staff of both parties, and take corrections from anyone. We do not endorse candidates or parties.

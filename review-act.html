Research Brief No. 3 · Washington

Who would benefit from the Review Act

The Review Act would let a judge take a second look at a long prison sentence and decide whether it still serves justice. About 3,035 people in Washington prisons would become eligible to ask, from 37 of the state's 39 counties.

How it works

A judge reviews the case. Nobody is released automatically.

People who have served a long sentence could petition the court that sentenced them. To get a hearing, a petition must show substantial rehabilitation or a minimal risk of reoffending. The judge then decides whether the original sentence still serves the interests of justice.

A review hearing can shorten a sentence. It can never lengthen one.

  • No release without supervision. Anyone resentenced serves at least five years of community custody.
  • Mandatory minimums hold. A judge cannot go below the mandatory minimum terms set in statute, such as 20 years for first-degree murder.
  • Exclusions. Aggravated first-degree murder and persistent offender ("three strikes") sentences are not eligible. Anyone convicted of first-degree murder must serve 20 years before petitioning.
  • Time to plan. No one can be released sooner than six months after the hearing.
  • No endless petitions. After a denial, a person waits three years and must show changed circumstances to file again.
  • Reasons on the record. The court explains its decision in writing.

For survivors

Victims are notified, heard and supported

  • Prosecutors must make reasonable efforts to notify victims and survivors of every hearing.
  • Victims and survivors may speak at the hearing, in person or through a representative, and the court must consider what they say.
  • A flexible fund at the Office of Crime Victims Advocacy would help with relocation, travel to hearings and therapy costs.
  • Victim advocates in prosecutors' offices would offer legal advocacy, safety planning, case management and restorative justice options.

Phased in over seven years

Who could petition, and when

  1. July 2027: people who have served 7 years for an offense committed at 17 or younger, and people who are terminally ill or have a permanent or degenerative medical condition.
  2. July 2028: 20 years served, for an offense at ages 18 to 24.
  3. July 2029: 13 years served, for an offense at ages 18 to 24.
  4. July 2030: adds 20 years served, for an offense at 25 or older.
  5. July 2031: 10 years served at ages 18 to 24; 17 years at 25 or older.
  6. July 2032: 13 years served, for an offense at 25 or older.
  7. July 2033 onward: 10 years served, for an offense at 18 or older.

At any time, a person may petition with the prosecuting attorney's consent. Emerging adults ages 18 to 24 have their own tier because brain development generally continues into the mid-20s.

What the evidence says

Washington has already tried this, on a small scale

Since 2020, Washington prosecutors have been able to ask a court to resentence someone. Through August 2023, prosecutors received more than 1,000 requests and filed 42 petitions, and by May 2024 courts had granted 41. For the 37 people with known sentences, total time fell from 731 years to 413. In 27 counties that received requests, prosecutors filed none.5

A judicial process gives every eligible person the same chance at review, wherever they were sentenced.

Of 98 people released early after long sentences for offenses committed as juveniles, 2 were convicted of a new felony.5 In Maryland, of 200 people released after an average of nearly 40 years in prison, 3.5% had a new conviction as of March 2024.6

Washington would build on a national movement: 15 states, D.C. and the federal system have some form of second look law. Washington is counted for prosecutor-initiated resentencing, which only a prosecutor can start.7

Read with care

What this brief does not show

  • Eligible is not released. These counts are people who could ask a judge for a hearing. A judge decides each case, and many petitions will be denied.
  • A 2024 snapshot. The count applies the 2025 bill's phases to the September 2024 prison population. People released or admitted since then, and changes in the 2027 bill, will move the numbers.
  • Two estimates. The Department of Corrections' 2025 fiscal note estimated 2,188 eligible people, using different assumptions. We will reconcile the two when the 2027 bill is filed.
  • Sentencing county, not home county. The county table shows where a sentence was imposed, which is not always where a person lived or will return.
  • The medical pathway is not counted. People eligible because of terminal illness or a permanent medical condition are not included.

01 · The ask

Put corrections dollars
where they do the most good

The governor projects a shortfall of about $4 billion in the first year of the next budget, and the Department of Corrections spends about $1.7 billion a year. There's room to spend that money better: supervising more people in lower-cost settings, giving people who have served long sentences a real review, and closing the units that empty out.

A graduated reentry slot costs a fraction of a prison bed. A commutation for someone serving life avoids decades of cost. Together with elder parole and unit closures, our preliminary estimate is about $70 million a year, or about $142 million a biennium, at an accelerated pace.

Five pieces

1

Grow graduated reentry

Supervise more low-risk people in the community, at a fraction of the cost. About 600 are in it now; full scale adds 2,000 more.

2

Commute at Inslee's scale

The governor follows his own board and publishes criteria for older and medically fragile people. Inslee commuted 422 during the 2020 COVID emergency.

3

Pass elder parole

Give people 50 and older who have served 20 years a hearing before the review board.

4

Make earned release retroactive

Higher earned release rates should reach people already inside, not just future offenses.

5

Close units as they empty

Re-close the Reformatory unit reopened in 2026 and close Monroe's solitary unit, moving people with serious mental illness to Sky River's treatment beds.

What we get back

Long sentences deter little

Deterrence comes mostly from the chance of getting caught, not from how long the sentence is.

Risk drops with age

Crime falls steeply as people get older, which is why long-serving older people deserve a structured chance at review.

The oldest cost the most

About 23% of people in state prisons are over 50 (DOC, March 2026), and their health care drives costs up.

04 · Who's in scope

Give people who've served long sentences
a real chance to show they're ready

This plan is for people who have served long sentences, especially older people and people with serious medical needs. It gives them a structured chance to show that keeping them locked up is no longer needed for public safety.

Who could qualify

  • People 50 and older who have served 20 years (elder parole)
  • People with serious medical needs or at the end of life
  • People under 18 at the offense, through the review board's existing petitions (under 21 if a bill like HB 1317 passes)
  • People nearing release who are assessed low risk (graduated reentry)

Who stays

  • Anyone the board finds likely to commit new crimes despite release conditions
  • People who don't meet a pathway's eligibility rules

Prison is still needed for some people.

05 · The package

Three tracks: an operating plan, a
bill package and executive action

The package runs on three tracks. They reinforce each other, but each can move on its own, and the savings depend on which ones pass.

ANear-term operating planBudget provisos · 2027–29
  • Unit closure plan. DOC names closure candidates with a plan for custody, receiving capacity, health care, staffing and one-time costs. Appropriations drop as units close.
  • Graduated reentry at scale. Funds 1,500 or more added graduated reentry slots, a lower-cost supervision option, along with housing vouchers and Medicaid enrollment at release, plus transfers, retraining and priority hiring for affected DOC staff.
BStatutory packageBills · 2027 session
  • Elder parole (proposed). Hearings before the review board for people 50 and older who have served 20 years; life-without-parole cases after 25 years. Builds on HB 1866 (2017–18).
  • Earned release, retroactive. A higher earned release rate that reaches people already inside. HB 1239 (2026) stalled, and its substitute covered only future offenses.
  • Concurrent enhancements. Lets judges run firearm and deadly weapon enhancements at the same time. Builds on HB 1178.
CExecutive actionGovernor · now
  • Commute at Inslee's scale: act on the four pending commutations, fund and fill the five new board seats from HB 1131 (2025), and publish criteria so older and medically fragile people get heard in batches. Medical placement can grow too (SB 5895, 2026).

Graduated reentry is a cost swap. Each move trades a prison bed for a slot costing about $13,500. Once units close, each one nets roughly $30,000 a year.

Commutations avoid future cost. A commuted life sentence avoids decades of prison cost, often $1 million to $2 million, partly offset by reentry and supervision.

Scale empties units. At 2,000 or more moves, whole units empty and can close. Savings per closed bed: about $45,000, below the $86,261 average because headquarters costs stay.

Small moves don't. A bed that stays open saves only about $7,424 a year, DOC's marginal cost in recent fiscal notes.

Possible avoided capital

If the Reformatory, its hospital and the solitary unit all leave the walled site, the $34.5M wall renovation and about $11M in other work could be dropped. Not counted above.

Timing

The population has held flat at about 13,900 to 14,000. Commutations and graduated reentry can move inside 2027–29; elder parole takes longer.

07 · Making the savings real

Tie population cuts to real
closures, and fund the transition

Tie closures to appropriations

When Larch closed in 2023, DOC used the savings to open units elsewhere. Savings reach the general fund only when the budget cuts the matching appropriation.

Let unit-level analysis pick the units

Facility averages mix custody level, health care and the people each prison serves. Closing one unit doesn't remove a prison's shared costs. DOC should cost each candidate unit.

Fund reentry before release

Housing, supervision and Medicaid enrollment at release. People who leave with nowhere to live are the likeliest to fail.

Take care of the workers

Many of these are union jobs. Plan transfers, retraining and priority hiring for other state jobs. Most Larch staff found work at other prisons or agencies.

08 · Objections and answers

What we'll hear, and how we'll answer

ObjectionAnswer
"This puts dangerous people on the street."Nobody is released just for being older. Elder parole and clemency need an individual hearing; earned release and graduated reentry use DOC risk tools, supervision and possible return to prison for violations. Page 5 lays out each pathway.
"Victims deserve to see the sentence served."Victims get notice, can take part in hearings and can ask for release conditions. Their input shapes the decision. The review asks whether more years in prison still serve public safety.
"Closing prisons kills rural jobs."Unit-level analysis comes first, and the plan funds transfers, retraining and priority hiring. Most Larch staff found new jobs.
"Releasing people doesn't really save money."Right, unless units close. That's why appropriations drop only as units close, and why we show the marginal cost next to the average.
"It's soft on crime."It's smart on crime. Research finds the chance of getting caught deters far more than sentence length. The plan spends part of the savings on reentry.
"Wait for a better budget year."The population over 55 keeps growing, and so does the cost per person. Waiting makes this more expensive.

One set of facts, different emphasis

Budget writers

A phased plan that saves money only as units close, with average and marginal cost side by side.

Swing legislators

Smart on crime: defined eligibility, individual review where it counts, and supervision after release.

Victims and advocates

Notice, participation and conditions stay. Their input shapes each decision.

Corrections staff

A transition plan before any closure: transfers, retraining and priority hiring.

09 · What if

Planning for the hard cases:
people, safety and savings

Every reform has a failure case. These are the ones legislators will ask about, with what the record shows and how the plan handles each.

What if someone who gets out commits a new crime?

What we know: In DOC's study of people released in 1998 and 2008, 4% to 5% of those 55 and older returned on a new crime within three years, versus about 22% of younger people. Of 73 people released through the board's juvenile process, followed six months to 11 years, one was convicted of a new felony (nonviolent eluding).

Our answer: Eligibility tiered by age, time served and risk assessment; release conditions and supervision; outcomes published every year.

What if graduated reentry participants fail?

What we know: Of 5,733 participants as of July 2026, 60% completed, 21% were terminated and 3.4% were counted as escapes.

Our answer: Violations can mean a return to prison. Fund rental vouchers and supervision, keep the substance use evaluation required since 2025, and report why people are terminated.

What about victims and survivors?

What we know: Victims already get notice and a voice in review board and clemency hearings.

Our answer: Notice and input at every release decision, release conditions victims can request, and part of the savings for the Office of Crime Victims Advocacy and survivor services.

Where will people live and get health care?

What we know: About 94% to 95% of people leaving DOC qualify for Medicaid. Washington's reentry waiver covers some services up to 90 days before release, launched July 2026. Graduated reentry can provide up to six months of rental vouchers.

Our answer: Pair every release pathway with housing funding and Medicaid enrollment, and count the federal match as an offset to DOC's medical costs.

What if the savings never show up in the budget?

What we know: DOC's budget grew while the prison population fell, and the Larch savings went to new units elsewhere.

Our answer: A budget proviso that cuts appropriations as units close, with quarterly reports to the fiscal committees.

10 · What if

Planning for the hard cases:
operations, labor and the law

The second set of questions is about operations, labor and the law. Each has a workable answer if it's built in from the start.

What if the prison population goes up?

What we know: The population has held flat at about 13,900 to 14,000, and the 2026 budget reopened a Reformatory unit at Monroe for 158 close-custody beds. The tight spot is custody level, not total beds.

Our answer: Warm closures with reopening triggers, for example occupancy above 95% of operational capacity for two quarters. Close by custody level, not just bed count.

What about staffing and overtime?

What we know: DOC custody staff worked more than 1.2 million overtime hours in FY2023, averaging about 23 hours of mandatory overtime a month.

Our answer: Closures redeploy staff to cut mandatory overtime. This is about fewer forced double shifts, not layoffs.

What about the unions and the law?

What we know: Teamsters Local 117 sued over the Larch closure; the court let DOC proceed in 2023, but the duty to bargain over effects remains. Clemency is the governor's constitutional power.

Our answer: Effects bargaining with Teamsters 117 and WFSE, transfer rights and lead time. The Legislature funds and requests clemency capacity; counsel reviews any closure proviso.

Do closed prisons still cost money?

What we know: Yes. Larch kept about 10 staff on site after closing. McNeil Island needed $2 million in emergency power repairs in 2025. Monroe's wall secures the Reformatory, its hospital and the solitary unit, so the wall project goes away only if all three move.

Our answer: Score savings after carrying costs. DOC has pledged to keep people with serious mental illness out of solitary; Sky River's treatment beds are on the same campus.

Can the system handle the workload?

What we know: Retroactive earned release means recalculating sentences. Elder parole needs review board hearings. Resentencing needs court time.

Our answer: Phase it in and fund DOC records staff, board hearing capacity and court time up front.

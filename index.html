The Northwest by the numbers

Three neighbors, three very different choices

Idaho, Washington, and Oregon share borders, families, and history. They do not share an approach to incarceration.

Higher than any democracy

Idaho locks up a larger share of its people than any independent democratic country on earth.

Idaho's prisons are full

About 10,000 people are held in Idaho prisons built for just over 8,200. The overflow goes to county jails and out-of-state beds.

Our approach

Evidence over ideology

We follow the data wherever it goes. When a policy keeps people safe at lower cost, we say so. When it fills prisons without making anyone safer, we say that too.

We work with lawmakers of every party. The best answers to crime and incarceration are usually found in communities: a job, a home, treatment that works, and people who show up. Our research asks whether the Northwest is investing in them.

What we're tracking

Idaho

In progress

A prison system over capacity

About 10,000 people in prisons built for just over 8,200.

The state now relies on county jails and out-of-state contract beds, and the Department of Correction is asking lawmakers for $45.3 million to open nearly 1,000 new beds.

Our question: What is driving the growth, and what would it take to reverse it instead of building more?

Source: Idaho Department of Correction via East Idaho News, Sept. 2026

Idaho

In progress

Women behind bars

152 per 100,000 women, the highest rate in the nation.

Neighboring Utah's rate was 28 in 2023. Idaho corrections leaders say about 80% of incarcerated women are serving time for drug or other nonviolent convictions, and that the root causes aren't well understood.

Our question: Which sentences and revocations put women in Idaho prisons, and for how long?

Source: Idaho Department of Correction via East Idaho News, Sept. 2026

Idaho & Oregon

In progress

Mandatory minimums

3, 5, or 10 years for fentanyl trafficking, set by quantity.

Idaho's 2024 law (HB 406) set those terms by quantity and took the decision away from judges. Oregon voters set mandatory minimums for many violent offenses through Measure 11 in 1994.

Our question: Who is being sentenced under these laws, and what do the extra years buy in public safety?

Source: Idaho Capital Sun, Feb. 2024

Washington

In progress

Long and life sentences

The nation's first three-strikes law, passed in 1993.

Washington has a lower incarceration rate than its neighbors, but long sentences keep people in prison for decades. A 2020 University of Washington report for the ACLU of Washington found long and life sentences fuel the state's incarceration.

Our question: How many people are serving sentences of 20 years or more, and who could safely come home through second look review?

Source: About Time, UW for ACLU of Washington, 2020

Also on our radar

Where we stand on related issues

Local jails

Jail expansion

Most people in local jails have not been convicted and are waiting for trial. Before counties build more beds, we urge them to fund pretrial services, treatment, and diversion.

Democracy

Voting after a conviction

Washington and Oregon restore voting rights on release from prison. In Idaho, rights return only after the full sentence, including probation and parole. People are often told the wrong thing, so we push for clear notice at release.

Redistricting

Prison gerrymandering

Counting incarcerated people where the prison sits, rather than at home, shifts political power to prison towns. Washington now counts people at home. We support that approach across the region.

Employment

Fair chance hiring

Washington and Oregon require most employers to wait before asking about records. Idaho does not. We support a statewide Idaho law.

See the state comparison

Families

Video visitation

Video calls should add to in-person visits, not replace them, and they should be affordable. Family contact is one of the best predictors of success after release.

Supervision

Probation and parole

Revocations for technical violations send people back to prison without a new crime. We want to know how often that happens in each state, and at what cost.

How we work

Our standards

  • Public sources first. We build on data from state corrections departments, courts, the Bureau of Justice Statistics, and public records requests.
  • Every number cited and dated. If you can't trace a figure back to its source, we haven't done our job.
  • Corrections in the open. When we get something wrong, we fix it and say what changed.
  • Lived experience as expertise. People who have been inside help shape our questions and check our conclusions.

About us

Founded in Boise in 2018. Working across the Northwest.

BarNone, Inc. is a nonpartisan 501(c)(3) research and policy organization focused on incarceration in Idaho, Washington, and Oregon.

Mission

We produce clear, independent research on incarceration and put it in the hands of people who can change policy.

Vision

A Northwest that uses evidence, not fear, to decide who goes to prison and for how long, and that welcomes people home when they return.

Our work has three parts: research on sentencing, prisons, and jails; policy work with legislatures and agencies in all three states; and community convenings and reentry resources that keep us grounded in the people these systems affect.

Our story

It started with a welcome

BarNone was founded in Boise, Idaho, in 2018 to offer grace to formerly incarcerated people and to advocate for and encourage them as they return to their communities. We believe a welcoming community full of open hearts and minds makes a world of difference for people working to rebuild their lives, and that getting them on their feet is in the best interest of everyone.

Our signature tradition was an annual release party welcoming returning citizens home, with dinner, motivational speakers, community resources, and prizes. Speakers came from all walks of life: people who had come home and thrived, public officials, corrections professionals, and authors.

From there we launched the Idaho Reentry Summit series, bringing lawmakers, the Idaho Department of Correction, the ACLU of Idaho, and people with lived experience together to talk about fair chance hiring and the future of Idaho's criminal legal system. We also published an Advocacy Toolkit for people working on reform.

Those years taught us that the hardest problems in reentry start long before release, in the laws that decide who goes to prison and for how long. Today BarNone is growing into a research and policy organization for the whole Northwest, studying those decisions across Idaho, Washington, and Oregon. We still keep our reentry directory, because research should never lose sight of the people it is about.

The guides below share what we've learned doing research and policy work. Use them to find the data, request the records, and make the case in Idaho, Washington, and Oregon.

Guide 1

Requesting public records

Every state gives the public a right to most government information. Getting it takes persistence. A good request is specific, polite, and cites the law.

StateLawFirst response due
IdahoIdaho Public Records Act, Idaho Code Title 743 working days, extendable to 10
WashingtonPublic Records Act, RCW 42.565 business days
OregonPublic Records Law, ORS Chapter 1925 business days to acknowledge
  • Ask for data in its original format, like a spreadsheet, not a PDF.
  • Name a date range and the specific records or fields you want.
  • Ask for a fee estimate before work begins.
  • Keep a log of every request, date, and reply.

Guide 2

Testifying at the legislature

Lawmakers remember stories. Two minutes from someone the bill affects can matter more than a stack of reports.

  • Find the hearing. Track bills at legislature.idaho.gov, leg.wa.gov, and olis.oregonlegislature.gov. Washington and Oregon offer remote testimony for many hearings.
  • Sign in early. Committees often limit time and close sign-up before the hearing starts.
  • Use a simple shape. Your name and town, your position on the bill, one story, one fact, and a clear ask.
  • Leave it in writing. Submit written testimony too. It becomes part of the record.

Guide 3

Writing that persuades

Our strategy depends on people reading what we write. Complex topics deserve plain language.

  • Lead with a person, then the policy.
  • Use one number, well explained, instead of five.
  • Use person-first language: "a person who was incarcerated," not "an ex-con."
  • End with one specific thing the reader can do.

Guide 4

Finding the data

Good data is one of the hardest parts of this work. These are sources we use most for policing, incarceration, probation, and parole.

  • Prison Policy Initiative: state profiles and national comparisons
  • Bureau of Justice Statistics: federal prison, jail, and supervision counts
  • Idaho Department of Correction, Washington DOC, and Oregon DOC: population reports and dashboards
  • Washington Caseload Forecast Council and the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission: sentencing data and forecasts

Write down where every number came from and when, so you can update it later.

Fair chance employment

A job is the strongest step home

Fair chance hiring, often called "ban the box," moves questions about criminal history later in the hiring process. People are judged on their qualifications first.

StateStatewide lawWhat it means
WashingtonYesThe Washington Fair Chance Act (2018) bars most employers from asking about criminal records until after they find an applicant otherwise qualified.
OregonYesSince 2016, most Oregon employers cannot ask about convictions on an application or before an initial interview.
IdahoNoNo statewide law for private employers. The Fair Chance Employment Act passed the Senate in 2020 but died on the House floor; a 2021 version was held in committee. Some Idaho employers ban the box voluntarily.

If you're looking for work

  • Practice a short, honest answer about your record that ends with what you've done since.
  • Ask local reentry programs which employers hire people with records.
  • In Washington and Oregon, an early question about your record may break the law. Legal aid can help.

If you're hiring

  • Remove the conviction question from your application.
  • Consider only records related to the job, and how long ago they happened.
  • Ask about the federal Work Opportunity Tax Credit and federal fidelity bonding for eligible hires.

Record relief

Clearing your record

A criminal record follows people into job interviews, rental applications, and school. Every state offers some way to limit that, but the rules differ a lot.

  • Washington lets many people vacate eligible convictions after a waiting period, and offers help with legal financial obligations.
  • Oregon allows many convictions and arrests to be set aside, and has expanded eligibility in recent years.
  • Idaho offers narrower options, so talk with a lawyer about what may apply to you.

Free legal aid organizations in each state, listed above, can help you check eligibility.

Mental health after prison

Post-Incarceration Syndrome (PICS)

Post-Incarceration Syndrome is a term some clinicians and researchers use for the lasting mental health effects of incarceration. It is not a formal diagnosis, but the experiences it describes are real and common.

People may notice:

  • Being on high alert, distrust of others, or trouble sleeping
  • Feeling overwhelmed by choices, crowds, or technology
  • Difficulty with emotions after years of keeping them hidden
  • Shame, isolation, or a sense of not belonging

What helps: a stable routine, peer support from others who have been inside, trauma-informed counseling, and treatment for any substance use. Families can help by being patient with small things and by asking what support feels useful.

If you are struggling right now,

call or text 988. You don't have to be in danger to reach out.

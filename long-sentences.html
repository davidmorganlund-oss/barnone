Issue explainer · Washington

Long and life sentences in Washington

Washington imprisons fewer people per resident than Idaho or Oregon. But the people it does imprison often stay for decades, and most have no way to ask anyone to review their sentence.

Published November 2026. For who would qualify under the leading reform bill, read our brief, Who would benefit from the Review Act.

Nearly half

of people in Washington prisons were sentenced to 10 or more years (28.6%) or to life (20.6%).1

41.5% in 2019

was the share serving 10 or more years when the University of Washington first measured it, with 17% serving life.2

More than 4 times

as many long, very long and life sentences were imposed in 2016 as in 1986, while violent crime fell.2

The problem

A low incarceration rate hides a long-stay prison system

A prison population is set by two things: how many people are sent to prison, and how long they stay. Washington sends relatively few people. It keeps many of them for a very long time.

In 1984 the Sentencing Reform Act abolished parole for nearly everyone sentenced after that date. Since then, the length of a prison stay has been set almost entirely by the sentence itself. Most people serving long sentences can earn only 10% to 15% off for good behavior, and none off any weapons enhancement.2

The result is an aging prison population. About 2,100 people in Washington prisons are 56 or older, and more than 300 are over 70.3 The risk of reoffending falls sharply with age, while the cost of care rises.

Long sentences also fall unevenly. Black people are about 4% of Washington's population but 18% of people in its prisons, and more than a quarter of people sentenced to life in the University of Washington's data. Native people are overrepresented at every sentence length.2,3

Compared with our neighbors

Fewer people in prison, but a far bigger share serving life

Washington's imprisonment rate is 181 per 100,000 residents. Oregon's is 291 and Idaho's is 490.4

How we got here

Sentencing laws, not crime, drove the growth

The University of Washington's 2020 report About Time traced the rise in long sentences to a series of policy choices made from the 1980s on.2

  1. 1984
    Sentencing Reform Act. Abolished parole for crimes committed after July 1, 1984, and made the sentence itself decide how long someone serves.
  2. 1986–99
    Offender score changes. Lawmakers repeatedly made prior convictions count for more. This was the single largest driver of long sentences.
  3. 1993
    Three strikes. Initiative 593 made Washington the first state with a three-strikes law, carrying mandatory life without parole. A two-strikes rule for certain sex offenses followed in 1996.
  4. 1994
    Auto-decline. Sent 16- and 17-year-olds charged with certain felonies straight to adult court.
  5. 1995
    Hard Time for Armed Crime. Added weapons enhancements of up to five years per count, served back to back and without good time.
  6. 2003
    Earned release limits. Capped good time at 10% for people convicted of serious violent offenses.

These laws also widened the gap between sentences after trial and after a plea deal. For violent offenses, people convicted at trial received sentences about 14.5 years longer on average in 2016.2

Since 2020

Reforms have trimmed the edges, not the core

  • Courts limited life without parole for young people. State v. Bassett (2018) barred it for anyone under 18. In re Monschke (2021) struck down mandatory life without parole for aggravated murder at ages 18 to 20.
  • Robbery 2 came off the strike list. The 2019 change was made retroactive in 2021, and people sentenced to life for it were resentenced.
  • Juvenile points stopped counting in adult offender scores in 2023, but only for new cases.
  • Prosecutors can ask for resentencing under a 2020 law. Through mid-2023 they received more than 1,000 requests and filed 42 petitions. Prosecutors in 27 counties that received requests filed none.6
  • Clemency has stalled. Since January 2025, the Governor has granted no commutations, including those the Clemency and Pardons Board recommended.7
  • Second-look bills have not reached a floor vote. The Review Act (HB 1125), a review pathway for people sentenced before age 21 (HB 1317) and retroactive removal of juvenile points (HB 1274) each cleared committee and stopped there in 2025 and 2026. See the record.

The evidence

People released after long sentences rarely return

Most people stop committing crimes as they age, usually by their 30s. That holds even for people convicted of serious violence.

  • Washington: of 98 people released after long sentences for crimes committed as juveniles, 2 had a new felony conviction.6
  • Washington: of people 55 and older released from prison in 2008, 4.1% returned on a new crime within three years.3
  • Maryland: about 200 older people released after decades in prison under the Unger decision have reoffended at very low rates.8

Survivors are not of one mind. National surveys find most crime victims favor shorter sentences paired with prevention and rehabilitation, and some Washington survivors have testified against second-look bills.9 Any review process has to notify survivors, hear them and fund the support they need.

What we don't know yet

Nobody publishes how many people are serving 20 years or more

The Department of Corrections groups every sentence over 10 years into one category. That leaves basic questions unanswered, and these are the ones we are working on.

Count

Who is serving 20+ years

How many people are serving sentences of 20 to 40 years, 40 or more, or life, and how long they have already served.

Drivers

What made each sentence long

How much of each sentence comes from enhancements, offender score and three-strikes law, and whether the case went to trial.

Equity

Native people, counted

National life-sentence data puts Native people under "Other." Washington needs its own count by race, tribe and county.

Aging

Older people inside

How many people over 55 have served 15 years or more, and what their care costs the state.

Review

Who gets a second look now

No state agency tracks prosecutor resentencing requests and outcomes. We want to.

Options

What each reform would reach

How many people would qualify under each proposal, from the Review Act to an age-and-time-served rule.

How we'll answer it. We are requesting de-identified, person-level records from the Department of Corrections: sentence length and parts, offense, age at offense, time served, race and tribal affiliation, sentencing county and conduct record. We will publish our methods with the results.
